# Design Anatomy — Taxonomy Foundation

## Purpose

Design Anatomy uses a research-grounded organizational taxonomy for its design library. The taxonomy is **our navigation model**, not a claim that the design industry has one universal hierarchy.

The model separates five different kinds of relationships that are commonly mixed together in design references:

1. **Foundations** — visual philosophies, structural traditions and historical movements.
2. **Surface & Material** — material metaphors, surfaces, depth and morphic treatments.
3. **Digital Interface** — interface visual languages, systems and UI strategies.
4. **Expressive & Creative** — colour, cultural/era, decorative and experimental visual languages.
5. **Motion & Interaction** — behavioural languages that can combine with any visual style.

## Classification rule

Every style should have:

- **Primary location** — where it fundamentally belongs.
- **Subfamily** — the closest meaningful relationship.
- **Type** — movement, philosophy, visual style, UI strategy, design system/language, etc.
- **Related concepts** — secondary relationships without forcing the style into multiple primary families.

This prevents future styles from being placed based only on naming, appearance, or convenience.

## Important distinction

Design Anatomy should never present its exact five-domain structure as an official industry standard. External research is used to validate the existence and relationships of styles; Design Anatomy supplies the navigational model that makes those styles easier to discover and compare.

## Future additions

Potential future styles should be classified first, then added to the library. Examples:

- Neubrutalism → Foundations → Raw & Anti-Refinement
- Liquid Glass → Surface & Material → Transparent / Fluid
- Spatial UI → Digital Interface → Spatial / Environmental
- Y2K → Expressive & Creative → Cultural / Era-Based
- Kinetic Typography → Motion & Interaction → Typography Motion

The taxonomy is designed so adding a new style does not require inventing a new top-level family unless research shows a genuinely new structural domain is needed.
